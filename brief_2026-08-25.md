**Selected: American Express is cutting credit limits**

Amex has quietly reduced "no preset spending limit" (NPSL) ceilings for some of its highest-spending personal cardholders over the past two weeks. Amex has long marketed NPSL as adaptive rather than fixed — recalculated continuously against spending patterns and risk models — so a reduction is contractually unremarkable on its own.

What's notable is the profile of affected accounts: long histories, high monthly spend, clean on-time payment records, tens of thousands of dollars removed overnight with no warning. Limit cuts typically follow missed payments or credit deterioration, not a functioning, healthy account — which is why the pattern, not any single case, is drawing attention.

Taken alone, one issuer trimming exposure on its highest-spend tier is routine portfolio management. Taken as a signal, synchronized tightening across "otherwise healthy" accounts resembles defensive moves lenders have made ahead of past downturns — commentators have drawn comparisons to 2007 and 2019, both pre-recession periods. Amex has not commented on any policy change.

Counterpoints: issuer risk models run continuously, not only pre-recession, so this could reflect a narrower fraud or category-specific tightening rather than macro caution; the reporting is also largely self-selected social-media accounts, not audited data.

Conversation starters:
1. If this is deliberate de-risking, what does Amex's typical high-spend customer profile suggest about where it expects stress to surface first?
2. Should "no preset spending limit" be marketed as flexible if the floor can drop overnight without warning?
3. Is anecdotal social-media reporting a reliable early indicator of credit tightening, or mostly selection bias?
