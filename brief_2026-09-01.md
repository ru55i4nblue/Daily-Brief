**Selected: Monaco hosts a bike race**

The Vuelta a España departed Monaco last weekend — making it the first place to have hosted the start of all three cycling Grand Tours — and the principality's opening time trial borrowed directly from its Formula 1 circuit, starting inside the casino and dropping through the marina's hairpins. Tadej Pogačar, a Monaco resident and the sport's dominant rider, won that stage by nine hundredths of a second, then took two more stages to build a near four-minute lead, closing in on the only Grand Tour missing from his collection.

On Saturday's Stage 8, a rock on the roadside sent him over the handlebars at speed. He left in an ambulance with a concussion, a broken collarbone and a neck fracture — his first abandonment in ten Grand Tours. His team says he is stable; the race continues without him, suddenly wide open.

Why it matters: a single roadside hazard has reshaped the season's last Grand Tour and put a rider widely regarded as the sport's best in an uncertain recovery window, with no return date confirmed. It also revives the recurring debate over rider safety on descents and technical stages, an issue cycling has repeatedly failed to resolve despite similar incidents in recent years.

Counterpoints: crashes of this kind are an inherent risk of professional road racing rather than evidence of a specific course failure, and Pogačar's dominance meant the Vuelta's competitive drama was already limited before the crash — his absence may simply produce a more open, not a diminished, race.

Conversation starters:
- Should Grand Tour organisers be doing more to mitigate roadside hazards on high-speed stages, or is this simply an accepted risk of the sport?
- How should a rider of Pogačar's stature manage recovery and season planning after a neck fracture, given the demands of the following year's calendar?
- Does his absence make the rest of this Vuelta more compelling to watch, or does it drain the race of its main storyline?
