**Selected: Creatine's surprising role in fighting cancer**

Creatine, long marketed almost exclusively as a gym-bag staple for building muscle and strength, is emerging in new research as a molecule with a role in cancer biology. Early studies point to creatine and the enzymes that process it being implicated in how certain cancer cells manage energy and survive metabolic stress, with some immune cells — notably CD8+ T cells that attack tumours — appearing to depend on creatine metabolism to function effectively inside the hostile tumour microenvironment. That has researchers exploring both ends of the mechanism: boosting creatine availability to strengthen anti-tumour immune responses, and in other contexts, blocking creatine pathways that some cancers hijack to fuel their own growth.

Why it matters: cancer immunotherapy's biggest limitation is that T cells often run out of metabolic fuel before they can clear a tumour. If creatine supplementation can measurably extend T-cell function inside tumours, it's a cheap, already-approved compound that could be repurposed as a low-cost adjunct to existing immunotherapies — a rare case of a supplement-aisle product having a plausible path into oncology protocols.

Counterpoints: most of this evidence is from mouse models and cell cultures, not human trials, and cancer's relationship with creatine is contradictory — some tumour types appear to use creatine metabolism to their own advantage, meaning a blanket "creatine helps fight cancer" framing could be actively wrong for certain cancers. Any real-world protocol would need to be cancer-type-specific, and self-supplementing based on early-stage research would be premature.

Conversation starters:
- If a supplement already sitting in most gym-goers' cupboards turns out to help immunotherapy work, what does that say about how underexplored "boring" compounds are in cancer research?
- Given creatine can apparently help both immune cells and some tumour cells, how do researchers even begin to untangle which effect dominates in a given cancer?
- Would you take a common supplement as an adjunct to treatment based on mouse-model evidence alone, if a doctor said it couldn't hurt?
