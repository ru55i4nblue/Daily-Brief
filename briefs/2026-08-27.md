	**Selected: The US Open ticket price backlash**

	The US Open's main draw begins 30 August, and the cost of getting in has become the story before a ball is struck. Grounds passes carry a face value of \$65 to \$135, but the May presale sold out within hours — nearly every pass available since has come through Ticketmaster's Verified Resale, the tournament's own official marketplace, where day-one prices hit \$363 this week. The pass covers only the outer courts and unreserved seating in Louis Armstrong Stadium and the Grandstand; Arthur Ashe Stadium is excluded entirely.

	Bill Ackman has led the public backlash, pointing out that the USTA is a non-profit whose stated mission is growing the sport, and asking how its marquee event ended up gated at roughly eight times Wimbledon's price — a grounds pass at the All England Club cost £33 (about \$45) this year, obtainable at that price simply by queuing. The Open was long tennis's most accessible major, the one a New Yorker could decide to attend that morning; that version is now largely theoretical.

	It matters beyond tennis: a non-profit sporting body extracting resale premiums through a marketplace it controls itself sits awkwardly next to a stated public-access mission, and the pattern echoes complaints levelled at other major events that route resale through in-house platforms.

	**Counterpoints:** dynamic resale pricing reflects genuine demand rather than manufactured scarcity, and USTA still funds grassroots tennis programmes regardless of how tickets change hands after the initial sale.

	**Conversation starters:**
	- Should non-profit sporting bodies face a different pricing standard than for-profit leagues?
	- Does routing resale through an official, tournament-owned marketplace change the ethics of the markup, since the USTA itself captures the premium?
	- Is \$363 for outer-court-only access still "accessible" tennis, or has the Open fully repositioned as a luxury event?
