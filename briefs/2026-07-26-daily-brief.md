**Selected: The Montreux Jazz Festival turns 60**

The Montreux Jazz Festival marks its 60th edition in 2026, having grown from a three-day jazz showcase founded by Claude Nobs on the shores of Lake Geneva in 1967 into one of the world's most eclectic music festivals, spanning blues, soul, rock and electronic acts well beyond its jazz roots. The anniversary is a moment to reckon with that drift: the festival's most famous cultural export isn't a jazz record at all but Deep Purple's "Smoke on the Water," written after a 1971 casino fire during a Frank Zappa show at the venue. Six decades on, Montreux functions as much as a Swiss Riviera tourism engine as a music event, anchoring the regional economy around Lake Geneva every July.

Why it matters: Montreux is a case study in how legacy cultural institutions survive by loosening the label that made them famous — the same tension playing out at jazz festivals worldwide as pure-jazz bookings struggle to fill arenas. Its 60th-anniversary programming doubles as a referendum on whether "jazz festival" still describes a genre or has become a brand covering any prestige live-music booking.

Counterpoints: purists argue the expansion into pop and rock headliners has hollowed out the festival's original identity and crowded out working jazz musicians who once built careers there. Defenders counter that Claude Nobs always booked broadly, and that genre fluidity is precisely what has kept Montreux solvent while narrower jazz festivals have shrunk or folded.

Conversation starters:
1. Does a festival owe loyalty to the genre in its name, or to whatever keeps it running?
2. Is Montreux's evolution a success story or a slow abandonment of jazz?
3. What would it take for a "pure" jazz festival to survive at Montreux's scale today?
