**Selected headline: Creatine's surprising role in fighting cancer**

Creatine, long sold as a cheap, well-studied muscle-building supplement, is now being investigated for a very different job: priming the immune system against cancer. UCLA researchers found that in mouse models of melanoma, creatine supplementation significantly slowed tumour growth and increased both the number and activation of dendritic cells — the immune cells that capture tumour fragments and direct killer T cells to attack them. The effect also held when human dendritic cells were tested with creatine in a lab dish, pointing toward potential uses either as a complement to existing checkpoint-inhibitor immunotherapies or as an ingredient in dendritic-cell cancer vaccines.

Why it matters: immunotherapy often fails not because the immune system is incapable of killing tumours, but because it isn't sufficiently activated against them. A supplement already established as safe in millions of gym-goers, if it reliably boosts dendritic cell activity in humans, would be an unusually cheap, fast-track addition to treatment protocols compared to a novel drug.

Counterpoints: a separate Nature Communications study this year found the opposite risk in a different context — exogenous creatine accelerated tumour metastasis via signalling between megakaryocytes and cancer cells — and outside experts noted that paper's own headline overstated what its data showed. Existing clinical trials of creatine in breast cancer patients focus on countering treatment-related muscle loss, not tumour suppression, and researchers are explicit that self-supplementing off the back of a mouse study is premature.

Conversation starters:
- If a supplement helps fight cancer in one study and accelerates it in another, how should doctors or regulators weigh conflicting early-stage evidence like this?
- Should promising mouse-model results ever justify off-label self-experimentation, given how often such findings fail to translate to humans?
- What would it actually take for you to trust an "immune-boosting" supplement claim — one high-profile study, or something more?
