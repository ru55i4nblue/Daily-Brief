**Selected: Garmin comes for Whoop's crown**

Garmin has entered the subscription-free wearable fight it had mostly sat out. In late July it opened orders for the Cirqa Smart Band — a screenless, fabric wristband (with an optional clip-on armband) that tracks heart rate, skin temperature, blood oxygen and sleep across more than 80 activities, rated for up to 10 days of battery life. It costs US$200 as a one-time purchase, with no subscription. That pricing model is the actual story: Whoop built its business on a mandatory monthly fee with no outright-purchase option, and Google's Fitbit Air had already started chipping at that model earlier in the year. Garmin, which already sells its watch lineup outright, is now applying the same no-subscription logic to the screenless "recovery band" category Whoop effectively created.

Why it matters: subscription fatigue is a real driver of wearable-buying decisions, and Garmin has the brand trust, retail distribution and battery-life reputation to make a serious dent in a category increasingly squeezed by cheaper trackers on one side and premium smart rings on the other. A credible no-subscription option, if its sensors prove as accurate as Whoop's, undercuts the pitch that justifies Whoop's recurring fee.

Counterpoints: Garmin's software and analytics layer has historically been less polished than Whoop's coaching and recovery-score UI — arguably the bulk of what subscribers are actually paying for. A one-time $200 also isn't necessarily cheaper over a full ownership cycle, and early leaks suggest some feature gaps against Whoop's newest hardware.

Conversation starters:
- Would you rather pay $200 once or a recurring monthly fee for a wearable, if the data quality were identical?
- Is Whoop's subscription model sustainable once major players like Garmin and Google offer hardware-only alternatives?
- Does bundling coaching and analytics behind a subscription make the data more valuable, or is it just a lock-in tax?
