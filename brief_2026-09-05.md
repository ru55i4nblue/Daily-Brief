**Selected: Monaco hosts a bike race**

The Vuelta a España set off from Monaco last weekend, making the principality the first place to have hosted the start of all three cycling Grand Tours. The opening time trial borrowed from the Formula 1 circuit, starting inside the casino and descending through the marina's hairpins; unlike the Grand Prix, watching from the barriers cost nothing. Tadej Pogačar, a Monaco resident and the sport's dominant rider, won the stage by nine hundredths of a second — an F1-tight margin on an F1 track.

He took two more stages and built a lead of nearly four minutes, closing in on the one Grand Tour missing from his collection. Then, on Stage 8, a rock on the roadside sent him over the handlebars at speed and out of the race in an ambulance — his first abandonment in ten Grand Tours, with a concussion, a broken collarbone and a neck fracture. His team says he is stable; recovery will take time even for cycling's dominant figure.

Why it matters: it turns a genuine cycling milestone — Monaco completing the trio of Grand Tour departures — into a footnote next to the abrupt removal of the sport's best rider from its most prestigious remaining prize, and reopens a race that looked settled after three dominant stages.

Counterpoints: a stray rock is a freak accident, not evidence the Monaco course or race organisation was unsafe — high-speed Grand Tour stages carry this risk regardless of host city. And "the one Grand Tour he hasn't won will have to wait" undersells how differently open the rest of the field now reads without him.

- Does a crash like this say anything about Grand Tour safety standards, or is it simply bad luck?
- How does Pogačar's absence change who the smart pick is to win this Vuelta?
- Is host-city prestige (Monaco completing the "all three Grand Tours" set) meaningful to fans, or mostly a marketing angle?
