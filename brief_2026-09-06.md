**Selected: Frieze Abu Dhabi and the great art land grab**

Frieze announced the 84 galleries confirmed for its first Abu Dhabi fair this week, locking in a new fixture on the international art calendar: 19–22 November at Manarat Al Saadiyat. Gagosian, Pace, Perrotin and Victoria Miro will show alongside the Gulf's established dealers, with sculpture programmes, artist awards and talks packed around the booths.

The fair caps two decades of state-directed culture-building. Abu Dhabi's culture department has spent that time turning Saadiyat Island into a museum district meant to rival any capital — the Louvre Abu Dhabi already open, a Guggenheim and the Zayed National Museum still to come — and it was the same department that brought Frieze in, folding its own 17-year-old homegrown fair into the bigger brand. The timing is pointed: Art Basel, the only fair with a stronger claim to global primacy, opened its own Gulf outpost in Doha this past February, putting the world's two biggest fair brands roughly 500km apart.

Why it matters: this is sovereign wealth buying cultural infrastructure at a pace private collectors can't match, and doing it in direct competition with a neighbour rather than in isolation — Abu Dhabi and Qatar are now racing each other, not just the West, for the art world's calendar.

Counterpoints: a fair with 84 galleries and A-list names doesn't guarantee sales — plenty of "instant" art hubs have struggled to build genuine collector depth beyond opening week. And two major fairs 500km apart in the same region may simply split a finite circuit of dealers and buyers rather than growing the pie for either city.

Conversation starters:
- Does Gulf money buying established fair brands (Frieze, Art Basel) change what those brands mean, or just where they show up?
- Will Abu Dhabi and Doha end up splitting the same collectors, or genuinely growing the market between them?
- What does "winning" look like for a state-backed art fair — sales, prestige, or something else entirely?
