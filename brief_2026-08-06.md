**Selected: Pogačar wins a fifth Tour, and breaks Alpe d'Huez**

Tadej Pogačar won the 2026 Tour de France, his fifth overall title, joining Jacques Anquetil, Eddy Merckx, Bernard Hinault and Miguel Indurain as the only riders to reach five wins. Along the way he took five individual stage wins, gifting two away to teammates and rivals, and delivered the tournament's signature moment on the Alpe d'Huez stage: a summit time of 35:27, breaking Marco Pantani's 1997 climb record that had stood for almost three decades. The win takes his career Tour stage-win tally to 26, nine behind Mark Cavendish's all-time record of 35.

Why it matters: the Alpe d'Huez record is one of cycling's most storied benchmarks, held by a rider from the sport's EPO-era who was later stripped of results elsewhere — Pogačar erasing it in the current, more heavily-tested era is being read as a marker of how far the ceiling on human climbing performance has moved. His fifth title also reopens the "greatest of all time" debate a full generation earlier than any of his four predecessors reached it.

Counterpoints: Pogačar's dominance has drawn recurring scrutiny over whether current anti-doping controls can meaningfully verify performances at this level, echoing (fairly or not) the shadow still cast by the sport's 1990s–2000s doping era. Critics also note a thinning of genuine GC rivals during his peak years makes cross-era comparisons harder, not easier.

- Does breaking a record set during cycling's doping era carry an asterisk, or does today's testing regime settle that question?
- Pogačar could plausibly chase a sixth or seventh title — would that put him clearly ahead of Merckx, or does era-adjustment make such rankings pointless?
- What's driving the current era's climbing times lower — equipment, training science, or something about the athletes themselves?
