**Selected: Atlas: a fitness tracker for the brain**

A San Francisco startup called Atlas has opened pre-orders for what it calls the first wearable that measures brain activity continuously, in daily life. The Atlas 1.0 is an adhesive patch worn on the hairless skin behind the ear, combining EEG electrodes with motion, skin-conductance and voice-vibration sensors — the last detecting that its wearer is talking without recording the words. The data feeds three live scores in an app: Presence, Stress and Energy Burn. Its claimed novelty is capturing usable brain activity while a person moves, walks or talks — something conventional lab EEG, which requires stillness, cannot do. Atlas says its algorithms are trained on more than 500,000 real-life brain recordings.

The Pioneer Edition costs \$499, including three months of membership; after that, a \$29.99/month subscription covers the app, analysis and replacement sensors (each lasting roughly two weeks). Shipping is estimated for Q1 2027. The company has raised \$24m from Acrew Capital and HOF Capital, and was selected for the NBA's Launchpad programme — but it carefully describes the device as a wellness product, not a medical one.

Why it matters: wearables spent 15 years quantifying the heart; if Atlas's mobile-EEG approach holds up, the brain could be next — with real implications for how people track focus, stress and burnout day to day.

Counterpoints: no independent validation of accuracy exists yet, wearable EEG from behind the ear is a much noisier signal than clinical scalp electrodes, and "no medical claims" is doing a lot of work to sidestep regulatory scrutiny of what are effectively health inferences.

Conversation starters:
- Would you trust a consumer patch to tell you something as intimate as your own stress or focus levels?
- Does "wellness, not medical" responsibly manage expectations, or dodge the accuracy bar the claims imply?
- If mobile brain-tracking takes off, who should own that data?
