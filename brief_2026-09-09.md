**Selected: Why St Kitts wants your fingerprints**

St Kitts and Nevis began issuing biometric passports this week, and for anyone holding citizenship through its investment programme, the change comes with a deadline: fingerprints and facial capture at an approved centre by 31 July 2027, after which old passports stop being valid for travel. Enrolment has been open since April, and appointment slots at some centres are already tight.

The driver is Europe. The European Commission has been watching the Caribbean's investor-citizenship programmes closely and has strengthened its ability to suspend visa-free Schengen access — the entire point of holding one of these passports for most buyers. St Kitts, the oldest such programme (dating to 1984, now a $250,000 minimum contribution), is the first to make the process deliberately less convenient, trading ease of acquisition for continued EU access.

Why it matters: citizenship-by-investment has operated for decades as a low-friction product — pay, wait, receive a passport. Biometric enrolment reintroduces friction and a paper trail, which is precisely what gives Brussels confidence the privilege won't be abused. Advisers expect the rest of the Caribbean's programmes to follow St Kitts' lead within a few years.

Counterpoints: biometric enrolment doesn't address the substantive concern critics raise about these programmes — that citizenship itself is for sale — it only adds identity verification. It's also unclear how rigorously the 2027 deadline will be enforced against existing citizens scattered globally, many of whom may never complete enrolment.

Conversation starters:
- Does adding biometrics make citizenship-by-investment more legitimate, or just harder to criticise?
- If the rest of the Caribbean follows St Kitts, does that strengthen or undermine the case for these programmes existing at all?
- Where should the line sit between a state's right to sell citizenship and the EU's right to gatekeep the access that citizenship buys?
