**Selected: The death of the airline lounge**

For roughly the past decade a single Priority Pass opened more than a thousand lounge doors worldwide, and it barely mattered which bank issued the card carrying it. That era is closing. Chase's Sapphire Lounges stopped accepting Priority Pass this week, ending both the annual free visit and the $75 walk-in rate for anyone arriving with a pass issued by another bank. The logic is the one Amex has applied to its Centurion Lounges since day one: the room exists to sell the card, so the card is what opens the door.

The lounge has become the decisive prize in banking's fight for premium customers. Revolut — a company that built its fortune on nobody ever needing to visit a bank in person — announced its first physical lounge this week, due at Copenhagen Airport in 2027 with access tied to its top subscription tiers. The reasoning is structural: a metal card finish or a cashback rate gets copied within a quarter, but a good space airside cannot be. Physical scarcity is the one differentiator competitors cannot clone.

The squeeze lands on the shared networks. Priority Pass is losing its best rooms one issuer at a time and is increasingly left offering an uninspired second tier. Anyone paying for that membership outright, or who selected a credit card primarily because of it, now holds a product materially worth less than it was a month ago.

**Why it matters:** it reframes lounge access from a travel perk into a competitive moat, and it is a live repricing — the value of an existing subscription fell without the subscriber doing anything or being asked.

**Counterpoints:** the "death" framing overstates it; lounge capacity is arguably growing, not shrinking, since every issuer building its own room adds seats to the system — the access is fragmenting, not disappearing. And the old model was arguably unsustainable: aggregated passes drove the overcrowding and queue-to-enter experience that made lounges worse for everyone, so exclusivity may be what restores the product.

**Conversation starters:**

- Is a lounge a genuine moat, or just the current round of an amenities arms race that ends in the same commoditisation as cashback?
- If access fragments across issuers, does the frequent traveller end up better off with more total lounges, or worse off needing several cards?
- Should a subscription product like Priority Pass owe compensation when its underlying network degrades mid-term?
