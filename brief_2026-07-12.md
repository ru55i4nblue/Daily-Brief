**Selected headline: Unilever weighs a bid for Thorne**

Unilever is reportedly exploring a bid for Thorne, a US supplements maker, in a deal that could value the company near $4 billion (per Financial Times reporting). Thorne, founded in 1984, makes dietary supplements — magnesium, omega-3s, ready-to-mix powders — and is on track for roughly $650m in annual revenue. Private equity firm L Catterton took the company private again in 2023 in a $680m deal and now appears to be shopping it around; consumer-health group Haleon is also said to be circling.

The interest fits a broader pattern: CEO Fernando Fernandez, who took over in March 2025, has been steering Unilever's portfolio toward beauty and wellness, adding Liquid IV, Nutrafol, Olly, SmartyPants and, as recently as April, Grüns. Thorne would be the biggest of these bolt-ons by a wide margin, giving Unilever a foothold in the higher-margin, practitioner-recommended supplement tier rather than mass-market vitamins.

Why it matters: consumer packaged-goods giants are increasingly betting that "wellness" — not laundry detergent or ice cream — is where growth and margin now live, especially as GLP-1 drugs and health-tracking wearables reshape how people think about supplementation. A $4bn Thorne deal would be one of the year's largest single wellness acquisitions and a signal that legacy CPG balance sheets are being redeployed wholesale into the category.

Counterpoints: the wellness-supplement space is crowded and lightly regulated, exposing acquirers to reputational and efficacy-claim risk. Roll-up strategies built on serial small acquisitions — five deals in under three years — can produce integration drag as easily as synergy. And it's only "exploring" a bid: Haleon or a financial buyer could win instead, or Thorne could simply stay private.

Conversation starters:
1. Is Big CPG's pivot into supplements a genuine strategic reinvention, or a hedge against declining trust in "clean label" packaged food?
2. Should practitioner-oriented supplement brands worry about losing credibility once absorbed into a mass-market conglomerate?
3. With five wellness acquisitions in three years, is Unilever building a coherent portfolio, or just buying growth wherever it can find it?
