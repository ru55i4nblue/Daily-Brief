**Selected: Creatine's surprising role in fighting cancer**

Creatine, long a staple sports-performance supplement prized for boosting short-burst muscular energy via the phosphocreatine system, has become an active subject of oncology research in 2026 as scientists probe its effects on immune cells and tumour biology far beyond the gym.

A UCLA study published in *iScience* found that creatine supplementation slowed tumour growth in mouse models of melanoma, increasing the number and activation of dendritic cells — the immune system's "coordinators," which flag tumour cells for destruction — and enhancing activation of human dendritic cells in lab dishes. This builds on earlier UCLA findings that creatine also fuels killer T cells, raising interest in creatine as a companion to dendritic-cell cancer vaccines and existing immunotherapies.

The picture isn't unanimous. A separate *Nature Communications* paper reports the opposite risk in a different context: exogenous creatine promoted metastasis in mouse models by altering megakaryocyte-to-cancer-cell signalling via a creatine kinase B–STAT5B pathway, accelerating the spread of already-established tumours.

Why it matters: the contradiction shows creatine's cancer effect is highly context-dependent — potentially helpful for priming certain immune responses, potentially harmful once metastatic signalling is already active. Human trials are underway (breast cancer survivors, ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04207359 and NCT05878106), but all current evidence remains preclinical — mice and cell cultures, not patients — so no dietary guidance follows yet.

Counterpoints: the immune-boosting and metastasis-promoting studies used different cancer types and models, so results may not generalise across cancers; and supplement dosing in humans differs substantially from the concentrations used experimentally in mice.

Conversation starters:
- Given the two studies point in opposite directions, should "cancer patients avoid creatine" or "creatine as adjunct immunotherapy" be the headline takeaway?
- How much weight should preclinical (mouse/cell) findings carry before any supplement guidance changes?
- Would you hold off on creatine pending human trial results, or does the immune-boosting angle justify trying it now?
