**Selected: The collapse of Harvey Nichols**

Harvey Nichols entered administration and exited almost immediately this week, sold to Frasers Group in a pre-pack deal for an undisclosed sum. The sale ends 35 years of ownership under Sir Dickson Poon, who bought the store from Burton Group in 1991 for £53.6m (~$68m), with roughly 1,200 jobs hanging in the balance at a retailer that has traded since 1831.

Beyond the department store sector's familiar decline, Harvey Nichols was wounded by policy: Britain scrapped VAT-free shopping for tourists in January 2021, leaving London the only major European luxury capital where visitors can't reclaim purchase tax — a permanent 20% price disadvantage against Paris. Retailers have lobbied the Treasury for five years to reverse it, and Harvey Nichols is now the argument's largest exhibit.

Why it matters: it's a case study in how policy, not just e-commerce or changing tastes, can hollow out a legacy retail category — and a test of whether a new owner (Frasers, which also owns Flannels) can hold the Knightsbridge flagship together while weaker regional stores face harder conversations.

Counterpoints: department stores worldwide have struggled for a decade regardless of VAT policy, so pinning the collapse mainly on the 2021 tax change may overstate one factor among several (rent, e-commerce, luxury spending shifts). The stores remain open and trading through the transition, so the "collapse" framing is more corporate-structure than storefront reality for now.

Conversation starters:
- Should Britain reverse VAT-free shopping for tourists to protect luxury retail, or is that subsidising a shrinking category?
- Does a pre-pack administration (sold same week it collapses) protect jobs better or worse than a slower wind-down?
- Is Frasers Group building a credible luxury stable (Flannels, now Harvey Nichols), or asset-stripping distressed brands?
